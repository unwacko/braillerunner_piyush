Why this matters

Braille needs a reader on both sides of the page

A blind child can write fluently in braille, but most of the people around them can't read it. Teachers, parents and examiners need it in print, in their own language, and in India that could be any of more than twenty. Braille Runner reads braille from one photo and turns it into 11 Indian languages, as text and as braille.

Rural schools

In a village school there is often no special educator for many kilometres. A blind student may sit in a regular classroom where the teacher has never learned braille, so their homework goes unmarked. With a phone photo, that teacher can read the work in Marathi, Odia or Kannada and grade it the same day.

Urban schools

Inclusive schools in cities enrol blind students next to sighted classmates, but resource teachers are stretched across many classes. Subject teachers, parents and classmates can read a braille answer sheet themselves. A student who writes in English braille can also share it with a grandparent who reads only Bengali.

People with no resources

Braille translation software, embossers and trained transcribers cost money that most families don't have. Braille Runner needs only a borrowed phone and a browser. A letter or note in braille becomes readable for the whole family, in their own language, for free.

Saving braille literature before the dots wear away

Braille books are pressed into paper, and the dots flatten each time a finger reads them. Handwritten braille notebooks, letters and poems, many of them in Indian languages, survive as a single copy in a school cupboard. Photographing and transcribing them keeps them alive, makes them searchable, and lets sighted readers find them too.

This matters because blind writers have shaped literature for thousands of years, and India's tradition runs deep: the bhakti poet Surdas, Sahitya Akademi winner Jagadguru Rambhadracharya, the memoirist Ved Mehta, and the composer-lyricist Ravindra Jain. Writers abroad belong to the same story, from Homer and Milton to Helen Keller and Borges.
